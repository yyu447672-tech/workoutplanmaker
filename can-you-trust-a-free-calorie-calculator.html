Which formula it uses, and why that matters

A useful calorie estimate should name the method behind it. WorkoutPlanMaker uses Mifflin-St Jeor as its default starting point, with Harris-Benedict and Katch-McArdle available for comparison. Naming those formulas lets you distinguish a published estimation method from an unexplained answer. A result presented without any named formula is a reason to withhold trust until the method is explained.

The calculation multiplies estimated BMR by an activity factor to estimate TDEE. The available factors are 1.2, 1.375, 1.55 and 1.725. That multiplication makes the activity choice consequential: the same resting estimate can lead to a different daily estimate when the assumed routine changes. Published formula sources explain the background, but they do not make your activity selection a measurement or turn a general calculator into an individual assessment.

What the number cannot tell you

A displayed calorie total is an estimate, not a direct measurement of your metabolism. Muscle mass, age, illness and medication can affect real energy needs in ways a short input form cannot fully capture. Different formula estimates can differ by a few hundred calories. Agreement between formulas is useful context, but it cannot prove that any answer matches your individual requirements.

Use weeks of weight tracking and your experience with your usual routine to assess whether an estimate is useful. A single day does not establish a reliable pattern. Recalculate when your daily routine changes materially, and keep the reason for the change clear. The purpose is to review an assumption against experience, not to promise a particular outcome or to make increasingly precise claims from the same limited inputs.

Five red flags in other calculators

An email address or payment demanded before you can see a basic result is a reason to inspect what you are agreeing to. A missing formula is another: without a named method, you cannot tell what generated the answer. A claim that the number is exact also deserves scrutiny, because an input form cannot directly measure your metabolism. These checks concern the claims and data handling, not a particular brand.

The remaining warning signs are a missing privacy policy and silence about limitations. Look for an explanation of where inputs go, what analytics are used and what the result cannot tell you. Free access alone does not establish trust, and an attractive result screen does not replace those explanations. Consider the signs together: transparent methods, understandable data handling and honest limits make an estimate easier to evaluate before you act on it.

Who should ask a professional instead

This is general fitness information, not a personal prescription. People under 18, people who are pregnant, people with medical conditions and anyone experiencing eating concerns should seek suitable professional guidance rather than treating a calorie calculator as an individual diet plan. The assessment’s adult estimates are for ages 18–80. For ages 16–17, the site provides only general exercise information and does not display BMI classification, BMR, TDEE, body fat, calorie targets, macros or meal plans.

A target below 1,200 kcal for women or 1,500 kcal for men triggers a warning; the site does not automatically raise the number. Those thresholds are warning signals, not goals, and a result above them is not a guarantee of safety or suitability. Automated arithmetic cannot determine whether a plan fits your health circumstances. Bring concerns about an estimate, symptoms or your relationship with eating to an appropriate professional instead of using the output to settle them.
