What MyFitnessPal is

MyFitnessPal is a food logging application. Its food diary helps you record what you eat, making it useful when your question is about meals already eaten rather than a target for tomorrow. A diary and a calculator answer different questions: one keeps a record of intake, while the other estimates a number to work toward. Choosing between them starts with deciding which of those tasks you need help with.

MyFitnessPal offers a free tier for basic logging and a paid Premium tier with extra logging features; check its own pricing page for the current split. WorkoutPlanMaker is an independent website with a different purpose. The two services are not affiliated, and using this calculator does not transfer a food diary or replace a record of your meals.

What WorkoutPlanMaker does instead

WorkoutPlanMaker calculates targets and builds workout plans. Its free macro, TDEE and protein calculators let you explore daily nutrition estimates without creating an account. Calculations run locally in your browser. Your weight, age and goals are inputs to those calculations; they are not entries in an online food diary. Getting a protein target does not tell the tool how much protein you actually ate today.

The complete four-week workout plan PDF is optional and costs a one-time $9. That purchase is for the PDF, with no subscription required. You can use the free calculators without buying it. Think of the outputs as starting estimates for planning, rather than measured energy needs or personalized medical advice. You still decide how to organize meals and how to keep any food record you want.

When MyFitnessPal is the better fit

Choose a food diary when your main goal is to record meals throughout the day. If you want to review breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks together, a tool designed for logging addresses that need directly. Recalculating a macro target after every meal would not create the same record: the target describes a plan, while the diary describes what you entered.

MyFitnessPal may therefore fit a routine built around regular food logging. Check its current product information before choosing a tier for a particular feature. You can also use WorkoutPlanMaker to estimate targets while keeping your meal records elsewhere. There is no need to treat an independent calculator as a replacement for every part of an existing logging routine.

When WorkoutPlanMaker is the better fit

Choose WorkoutPlanMaker when you want a starting calorie estimate, daily macro targets and a workout planning option without signing up. This approach suits someone who wants to see the planning numbers first and decide separately whether to keep a food diary. It also keeps the free calculation separate from the optional PDF purchase, so a calculator visit does not require a paid plan.

Browser calculation does not mean the website has no analytics. The site uses Google Analytics and Microsoft Clarity for visits without collecting your body details. Read the privacy policy for how fitness inputs and print files are handled. If your priority is an ongoing meal history, remember that this site does not store that history for you.

Switch in three steps

First, use the TDEE calculator to estimate your daily calorie budget. Treat the result as a planning estimate, with the activity choice reflecting your usual routine. Second, use the macro calculator to turn the calorie target into protein, fat and carbohydrate targets. For fat loss, this site's protein setting is 2.0 g per kg of body weight. These outputs describe daily goals, not a log of food consumed.

Third, optionally get the four-week workout plan PDF if you want that training document alongside the estimates. Keep a separate food diary if recording meals remains useful to you. Switching your target calculator does not require abandoning your existing notes or meal records; it simply changes where you calculate the numbers used to plan your day.
