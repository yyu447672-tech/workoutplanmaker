What TDEE measures

TDEE describes total daily energy expenditure: the energy used across a day, including rest, ordinary movement and exercise. It answers a budget question rather than a food choice question. A TDEE result helps you understand the scale of your daily energy needs before deciding whether your eating plan is intended for maintenance, fat loss or muscle gain.

The TDEE calculator uses Mifflin-St Jeor as its starting estimate and offers Harris-Benedict and Katch-McArdle for comparison. Estimated BMR is multiplied by an activity factor selected from 1.2, 1.375, 1.55 and 1.725. Most adults land between 1,800 and 2,800 kcal a day, but that broad range is context, not a personal target. Your result still needs to be checked against your actual routine.

What macros measure

Macros describe how a calorie budget is divided among protein, fat and carbohydrates. They answer a composition question: what does that energy allowance look like in grams of each nutrient? A macro result does not establish how much energy you burn. Its usefulness depends on the calorie target supplied to it and the goal you choose.

This tool uses protein at 2.0 g per kg for fat loss, 1.6 for maintenance and 1.8 for muscle gain, with fat at 0.9 g per kg. Carbohydrate fills the remaining calorie allowance. Protein and carbohydrate provide 4 kcal per gram, while fat provides 9. These are the calculator’s planning assumptions, not a personalized nutrition prescription or proof that a particular split is right for every adult.

The order: TDEE first, macros second

When you do not already have an energy target, start with the TDEE calculator. It supplies the context that makes a macro split meaningful. Then use the macro calculator to explore the composition of the target you have chosen. The tools address connected questions, so using both does not mean adding their results together or treating either result as extra calories to eat.

The full example in the how-to-calculate-macros guide uses 70 kg and 1,800 kcal to give 140 g protein, 63 g fat and 168 g carbohydrate.

Follow that guide for the worked calculation rather than trying to reconstruct it from a comparison page. Here the important distinction is the role of each result: TDEE provides the energy reference, while macros describe the distribution within an eating target. A neatly balanced set of grams cannot validate an unsuitable energy budget.

When you only need the TDEE calculator

Use just the TDEE calculator when your question is about overall energy needs. You may want a reference for maintenance, a way to review an existing calorie deficit plan, or a check on whether an activity choice matches your ordinary week. Those questions do not require a detailed protein, fat and carbohydrate breakdown to become useful.

Keeping the task focused can also make a result easier to interpret. If your goal is simply to understand a calorie budget, generating more numbers does not automatically improve the decision. Review the assumptions behind the estimate and how they fit your routine. You can return to the macro calculator later if planning meals in grams becomes useful.

When you only need the macro calculator

The macro calculator can stand alone when you already have a calorie target from a coach or an established plan. Entering that target lets you inspect its protein, fat and carbohydrate allocation without asking a separate calculator to choose a different energy budget. The source of the calorie number still matters: a convenient input is not necessarily an appropriate one.

Keep the goal consistent with the plan you are checking. If a professional has given you individual guidance, a generic calculator should not silently replace it. Use the output to understand the site’s assumptions or to prepare questions about your plan. Neither calculator records meals, so target grams should also be distinguished from what you actually eat.

Mistakes that waste the numbers

Choosing the highest activity factor because it produces a more appealing budget can undermine the estimate before you use it. Match activity to your usual life rather than an unusually busy day or a hoped-for training routine. Likewise, a macro percentage without a calorie total leaves the actual gram target unclear; percentages alone do not answer the energy question.

Treating an estimate as a prescription and changing targets every day are other ways to lose the context. Keep track of the assumptions you used and review your experience over weeks instead of reacting to every result. Revisit the energy estimate when your routine changes materially. If the numbers conflict with individual professional advice, take that conflict to the professional rather than choosing whichever calculator result looks easier.
